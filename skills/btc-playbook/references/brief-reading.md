# Reading a brief

## Where the case round sits
Typical campus flow: CV shortlist → case submission → interviews. The case round is usually the
sharpest cut. Two consequences:
1. The submission must survive a **two-minute skim** before it earns a twenty-minute read.
2. The case keeps mattering after you clear it: interviews are built on it ("why this feature
   over that one?", "where did this number come from?", "what if this assumption is wrong?").

## Three layers of every brief
1. **Explicitly asked**: named deliverables and coverage areas. List them; each becomes a section.
2. **Implied**: what the ask quietly requires. "Hit a revenue target by year 3" → a financial
   model. "Choose one of three modules" → the choice itself is evaluated, and synergies with the
   unchosen ones may be expected. A glossary in the brief → domain knowledge must be acquired.
3. **Deliberately left open**: choices the brief hands you (module, path, persona, topic). The
   choice is being evaluated; justify it with stated criteria.

Also extract: hard limits (slides/pages/words/font size/time), required sections, evaluation
parameters if published, submission format, and any "you may argue it is a bad idea" permission.

## Precedents, parallels, contrasts (before generating solutions)
- **Precedent**: has this exact thing been tried, by this company or another? What happened?
- **Parallel**: where was a structurally identical problem solved in another industry?
  (e.g., price parity between aggregator and direct prices: hotels and airlines fought it for years.)
- **Contrast**: where does the analogy break? This is where judgement shows.
  (e.g., delivery merchants are far more fragmented and price-sensitive than hotels, and parity
  clauses draw competition-law scrutiny.)
- Check what the company **already runs** before proposing it. Proposing an existing feature is a
  common, avoidable failure.

## Hypothesis first
Decide roughly what you believe, lay out the argument that would support it, then research to
fill gaps or change your mind. Open-ended research hoping an answer emerges eats the timeline.

## Choose the less obvious angle
The easy interpretation is the one most entries take. A defensible harder angle stands out
immediately. Bring your own work experience into the analysis explicitly; a lens you actually
developed carries weight and is underused.

## Scope to the clock
Under a short window the skill is **selection, not compression**. Cover four things properly and
say why the others were out of scope; eight things gestured at reads worse.
